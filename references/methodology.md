# Method selection for article reading

This guide supplies questions to ask, not a fixed scoring algorithm. The methods below were developed for different purposes; their combination here is a practical reading design, not a separately validated scientific protocol. Select a lens after reading enough of the article to know what it is trying to do.

| Article function | Primary questions | Useful lens | Common mistake |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Explanatory or opinion essay | What problem, claim, reasons, and qualifications organize the text? | Rhetorical relations and Toulmin-style argument elements | Treating a vivid example as proof of a general claim |
| Technical tutorial | What prerequisites, mechanism, steps, result, and environment are needed? | Causal/process reconstruction | Calling a recipe reproducible without version, inputs, or verification; treating an untested interactive demo as observed evidence |
| Experience report | What was observed, under which conditions, and what was inferred from it? | Observation–interpretation separation | Generalizing one team's experience to all contexts |
| Business or product analysis | Whose objective and incentives matter? Which data, tradeoffs, and alternatives support the recommendation? | Argument and decision analysis | Treating a proposed strategy as an observed outcome |
| Philosophical essay | How are key terms defined? Which premises lead to which conclusion, and what counterexample matters? | Concept and argument analysis | Importing a familiar definition the author never adopted |
| Information collection | What is gathered, what is selected or omitted, and what does the author actually conclude? | Source and selection analysis | Inventing a unifying thesis where none is stated |
| Translation or secondary synthesis | Which claims belong to the original source, the translator/compiler, or an added practice note? | Source attribution across layers | Assigning an added recommendation to the original author |

## Four complementary lenses

1. **Text structure.** Map the relations between sections: background, problem, method, evidence, contrast, implication, or conclusion. Rhetorical Structure Theory is one source for this kind of relation analysis. Do not demand a textbook structure from narrative or fragmentary writing.
2. **Argument structure.** For a substantive claim, identify evidence, the reasoning that connects it to the claim, scope qualifiers, and possible rebuttals. Toulmin's model is a useful vocabulary; do not run every paragraph through all elements.
3. **Text versus situation model.** Separate information actually in the article from the reader's background model needed to make sense of it. External research may improve the latter but cannot be attributed to the author.
4. **Learning check.** When the user is studying, ask them to explain a relationship, predict what changes under different conditions, or apply an idea to a small case. Self-explanation and retrieval studies motivate this optional step; a quiz is not the default deliverable.

## Match claims to evidence

Track two questions separately: **who says this?** and **what would establish it?** The source label in `SKILL.md` answers the first at a broad level; a translated or annotated article may require naming both the original author and the annotator. The second depends on the claim:

- **Mechanism or procedure:** identify inputs, environment, version, steps, failure conditions, and the output the article says to expect. Code shown in text is a proposed procedure; printed output is an article-reported result; an independent run requires an actual run in a stated environment.
- **Empirical or causal claim:** identify the observations, comparator, measurement, uncertainty, and possible alternative explanation. A behavior change is not necessarily a correctness check; a case is not a population estimate.
- **Conceptual distinction:** separate a source's definitions from the article author's proposed mapping or synthesis. Ask which counterexample would break an alleged equivalence.
- **Decision recommendation:** identify the immediate artifact of the proposed process, the outcome the reader cares about, and the evidence connecting them. A team's agreement, a hypothesis, customer feedback, and market success are different stages.

Ask only the questions that could change this article's interpretation or the reader's decision. Walton's critical-question approach motivates tailoring objections to the type of reasoning; multiple-document sourcing research motivates keeping claims tied to their origin; evidence-based management distinguishes research, local data, practitioner experience, and stakeholder views for decisions. These are lenses, not a validated score or a demand to collect every evidence type for every article.

## Choosing depth

- **Quick orientation:** one-sentence topic and position, brief reasoning path, one or two important limits.
- **Study:** add concept relationships, prerequisites, and a small application or self-explanation prompt.
- **Critical review:** examine consequential claims, evidence quality, alternatives, and targeted external checks.

These are response depths, not separate hard-coded Skill modes. Match the user's request and the article's actual complexity.
